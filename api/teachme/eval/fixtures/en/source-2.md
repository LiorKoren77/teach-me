# The Water Cycle (continued)

## Precipitation

Inside a cloud, droplets collide and merge. When a droplet grows heavy enough that rising air can no
longer support it, it falls. Depending on the temperature of the air it passes through, it reaches
the ground as rain, snow, sleet or hail. All of these are precipitation, and precipitation is the
only way water returns from the atmosphere to the surface.

Precipitation is very unevenly distributed. Mountains force approaching air upwards, so their windward
slopes are wet while the land in their shadow may be desert. Deserts and rainforests can lie at the
same latitude for this reason.

## Collection, runoff and groundwater

Water that reaches the ground takes one of three paths. Some evaporates again almost immediately.
Some flows downhill across the surface as runoff, gathering into streams and rivers that eventually
reach the sea. The rest soaks into the soil, a process called infiltration, and moves slowly
downwards until it fills the spaces between grains of rock. That buried water is groundwater, and
the level below which the rock is saturated is the water table. Wells reach water by being dug below
it.

Groundwater moves, but very slowly - centimetres a day rather than metres a second - and it may stay
underground for thousands of years before it seeps into a river, a spring or the sea. Snow and ice
store water for even longer: a glacier holds water for centuries, and the ice sheets of Antarctica
and Greenland hold the great majority of the planet's fresh water. These stores are what make the
cycle steady enough to rely on: rain is erratic, but rivers fed by groundwater and melting snow flow
all year.
